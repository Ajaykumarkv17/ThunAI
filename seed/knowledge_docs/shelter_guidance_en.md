# Community Shelter Guidance

When a flood forces you to leave your home, a community shelter is meant to be
safe, calm, and organised, even when it is crowded. This guide explains what
to expect and how to make the stay easier for your family and for everyone
sharing the space.

## Deciding when to move to a shelter

- If a coordinator, a responder, or an official alert tells you to evacuate,
  do not wait to "see how bad it gets." Water levels near a river reach can
  rise faster than they appear to from a distance.
- Move to a shelter earlier rather than later if your household includes
  young children, elderly members, pregnant women, or anyone with a mobility
  difficulty or a chronic illness. Moving in good light and calmer water is
  much safer than moving after dark or once water is waist-deep.
- If you are unsure whether to evacuate, contact your ward coordinator or the
  helpline for guidance rather than deciding alone.

## What to expect at the shelter

- On arrival, you will normally be asked to register: your name, the number
  of people in your household, and any urgent medical or mobility needs. This
  helps the shelter team allocate space and plan food and water supplies.
- Shelters are typically organised in schools, community halls, or places of
  worship with open floor space. Expect shared sleeping areas, shared
  sanitation facilities, and a designated area for cooking or food
  distribution.
- Mobile network congestion is common when many people gather in one place
  during a flood event. Keep messages to family short, let them know you have
  reached the shelter safely, and avoid non-essential calls that use up
  limited network capacity others may need for emergencies.
- Power cuts are common during flood events. Shelters may rely on generators
  or battery lighting for part of the day; bring your own torch and spare
  batteries or a power bank if you have them.

## Bringing family, animals, and belongings

- Bring identification documents if you can safely collect them (ration
  card, Aadhaar, or any ID you hold), since these help with registration and
  with any later relief distribution.
- If you have pets or livestock, ask the shelter coordinator about the local
  arrangement before arrival where possible; many shelters have a separate
  designated area for animals rather than allowing them inside the main
  sleeping area.
- Keep valuables and essential medicines with you rather than left behind or
  packed at the bottom of a bag; you may need medicines within the first few
  hours.

## Looking out for one another

- Check on neighbours, especially elderly residents living alone, before you
  leave for a shelter. A short knock to ask "are you going to the shelter
  too?" can be the difference for someone who cannot move quickly.
- If someone in the shelter needs mobility assistance, tell the shelter
  coordinator or a responder rather than assuming someone else has noticed.
- Shelters work best when people share space calmly. Keep shared areas,
  especially sanitation facilities and cooking areas, clean for the next
  family who will use them.

## If a shelter reaches capacity

- If the nearest shelter is full, a responder or the coordinator will direct
  you to the next available shelter. Do not assume a full shelter means no
  shelter is available; ask before deciding to stay in an unsafe location.
- Shelter capacity and current occupancy are tracked by the coordination
  team so that arrivals can be redirected quickly; this is why registering on
  arrival matters even when the shelter feels informal.

## Quick checklist

- [ ] Move early, especially with children, elderly, pregnant, or mobility-
  limited household members
- [ ] Register on arrival with household size and any urgent needs
- [ ] Bring ID documents and essential medicines with you
- [ ] Bring a torch or power bank in case of power cuts
- [ ] Keep phone messages short to preserve network capacity for others
- [ ] Check on neighbours before leaving, especially those living alone
